After Zhou Yuan moved out, no one called him for a long time.
He lived alone in a very small apartment, far from the office, but cheap.
At night he ate alone, watched TV alone, with no need to say a word.
He spent three months like this.
His mother still called often.
"When are you coming home?" she asked on the phone. "You're alone in Beijing, I don't feel at ease."
"I'm fine, Mom."
"I still don't understand you and Xiaoyu. You were together five years — how do you just break up like that?"
Zhou Yuan said nothing. It wasn't something he could explain to his mother, and he didn't want to try.
"If you're sad, come home," his mother said. "What's so good about Beijing?"
"I'm used to it," he said.
He hung up and sat at home, looking at the dark television.
His phone rang again. This time it wasn't his mother.
A number he hadn't been in touch with for three years: Su Ting.
"You there?" Her text was only two words.
Zhou Yuan looked at those two words for a long time.
He remembered who Su Ting was: Xiaoyu's best friend, a friend of eight years, someone he'd exchanged fewer than twenty sentences with.
He didn't know why she was contacting him.
He typed: "Here."
A moment later, his phone rang again.
"Are you okay?"
Zhou Yuan looked out the window. Beijing's night was quiet, the streetlights below lit up one by one.
He thought for a long time before answering: "I don't know. You?"
Su Ting replied quickly: "I don't know either."
"Come out for a drink?" she wrote next.
Zhou Yuan looked at that line, and something moved in his chest.
He didn't know what it was.
"Okay," he typed. "You pick the place."
The bar was small, the lights dim. Su Ting was already sitting inside, a drink in front of her.
"You've gotten thin," she said.
"So have you."
Zhou Yuan sat down and ordered a beer.
The two of them were silent for a moment.
"How's Xiaoyu these days?" he asked first.
Su Ting lit a cigarette. "You actually want to know?"
"Not really," he said, and laughed at himself.
"She changed companies, runs her own team now," Su Ting said. "The pay isn't great, but she seems happy."
"And Chen Mo?"
"Gone. Went south. The thing with his wife got ugly."
Zhou Yuan drank some beer and said nothing. He'd expected to feel sad. He didn't.
"Why did you look for me?" he asked.
Su Ting looked at him for a long time without speaking.
"Because no one ever asked if I was okay," she said finally. "I spent three months crying with Xiaoyu, and no one was there for me."
"You're sad too?"
"I'm not sad about Xiaoyu's thing," Su Ting said. "What I'm sad about is…" She stopped and drank.
"What is it?"
"Nothing." She smiled — a strange smile. "Do you remember when we first met?"
Zhou Yuan thought about it. "Five years ago. You brought Xiaoyu to a friend's party. That's when we met."
"Right," Su Ting said. "You bought me a drink that day."
"I don't remember that."
"I do," she said, her voice quiet.
The second time they met, it was Su Ting introducing Xiaoyu to him.
"Actually," Su Ting said, "I knew you first."
Zhou Yuan froze. "What do you mean?"
"That party five years ago, we talked all evening." Su Ting lit a cigarette, her hand trembling slightly. "I thought you were a good person. Honest, quiet, not like other men."
"And then?"
"And then I introduced you to Xiaoyu."
The bar was quiet, only the music playing.
"Why?" Zhou Yuan asked, his voice a little cold.
Su Ting looked at her glass, not lifting her head for a long time. "Because Xiaoyu had just broken up with someone, and she was hurting. I thought she needed a good person."
"And you? Didn't you need one too?"
"I did," she said. "But I thought she needed it more."
Zhou Yuan fell silent. He thought back to the stranger she'd been five years ago — the first time they met, she'd told him her name was Su Ting.
"Do you regret it?" he asked.
Su Ting raised her head, her eyes red. "What do you think?"
She said nothing more, and finished the wine in her glass.
"I should go," she said, standing up. "I've said too much tonight."
Zhou Yuan grabbed her hand. It was the first time he'd ever touched her.
"Wait," he said.
Su Ting stopped, without turning around.
People at the company were still talking about Chen Mo.
"Did you hear?" someone said. "Chen Mo left, went south. Sounds like it's not going well for him."
"He brought it on himself," another said. "Lying to a girl — in the end he only lied to himself."
Zhou Yuan was passing by and overheard. He stood there, not moving, for a long time.
He hadn't expected that hearing this news would bring him no hatred at all — only a discomfort he couldn't name.
That night, he messaged Su Ting: "Chen Mo's gone. Did you know?"
"I know," she replied. "Xiaoyu didn't tell you?"
"We don't talk anymore."
"Oh."
"Come out for a drink?" he asked.
This time, he was the one who asked first.
At the bar, Su Ting drank faster than usual.
"Those four years you were married," Zhou Yuan asked, "was it like this too? Looking fine on the outside, but nothing right inside?"
Su Ting smiled once, an ugly smile. "How would you know?"
"Because I'm like that right now."
"You have a secret too?"
"I don't have a secret," Zhou Yuan said, looking at her. "I just don't know what I'm waiting for."
Su Ting said nothing, lit a cigarette, her fingers trembling a little.
"When I got married," she said, "everyone said I'd found a good man, that I should be happy. I believed it myself too. But it took me four years to realize I never really liked him."
"Then why did you marry him?"
"Because he was safe," Su Ting said. "Because with him, there was nothing to be afraid of."
She lifted her head and looked at Zhou Yuan, something in her eyes he couldn't read.
"You know what the scariest part is?" she said. "It's discovering you've always been looking for safety, and never once looked for something real."
They spent several nights like this.
Each time it was drinking, talking, then going home.
But that night was different.
The rain was heavy, and taxis were hard to find.
"I'll take you home," Zhou Yuan said.
"My place is far."
"That's fine."
In the car, neither of them spoke. Rain hit the windows, loud.
When they reached Su Ting's building, she didn't get out.
"Come up for a bit?" she said. "It's pouring outside."
Zhou Yuan looked at her. He knew what that sentence meant — someone else had said almost the same thing, three years ago.
"We shouldn't," he said.
"I know," Su Ting said. "But I don't want to be alone."
He'd heard that line before. He'd said the same thing to Xiaoyu once himself.
He followed her upstairs.
The apartment was small, tidy — like her — and empty.
Su Ting poured two glasses of wine, her hand still shaking.
"You're shaking," he said.
"I haven't felt like this in a long time," she said. "Nervous."
"Nervous about what?"
"Nervous about wanting something."
Zhou Yuan walked over, took the glass from her hand, and set it on the table.
"Su Ting."
"Mm."
"Have you wanted this since five years ago?"
Her tears suddenly fell. "Yes. Five years."
Zhou Yuan reached out and wiped the tears from her face.
This time, neither of them stopped.
In the morning, Zhou Yuan woke early.
Su Ting was still asleep, looking younger than usual.
He looked at that face. He felt no regret — only a great fear.
He thought of Xiaoyu. Not sadness, something else — like he'd betrayed something, but Xiaoyu wasn't his anymore, so what kind of betrayal was this even?
But Su Ting was Xiaoyu's best friend. That was the real betrayal.
Su Ting woke up and saw him looking at her.
"What are you thinking about?"
"Xiaoyu," he said honestly.
Su Ting's face fell a little, but she wasn't angry.
"I know," she said. "I'm thinking about her too."
"We can't tell her."
"I know." Su Ting sat up. "You think I don't know? Eight years of friendship, and I still…" She didn't finish.
"Do you regret it?" Zhou Yuan asked.
Su Ting looked at him for a long time. "I should say I do."
"But?"
"But I don't," she said. "These five years — this is the first time I've felt real, instead of performing."
Zhou Yuan fell silent.
"We can't do this again," he said.
"I know," Su Ting said.
But neither of them moved. Neither of them got out of bed.
Over the next two months, neither of them mentioned that night again.
But they saw each other again — seven times, eight times, Zhou Yuan couldn't say for sure.
Every time, they said it was the last time. It never was.
Su Ting grew afraid of seeing Xiaoyu.
Once, the three of them nearly crossed paths — Xiaoyu said she'd come to Su Ting's for dinner, and Su Ting said she wasn't feeling well and didn't go.
After hanging up the phone, she sat on the floor and cried for a long time.
"Why are you crying?" Zhou Yuan asked her later.
"Because I realized — introducing you to Xiaoyu five years ago wasn't because I didn't want you," Su Ting said, her voice quiet.
"It was because I thought you should be with her, not me."
Zhou Yuan froze.
"What I told her that night," Su Ting went on, "that I regretted taking four years to admit I wasn't happy — that part was true."
"But I also wanted her to leave," she said. "I thought, if she left…"
"You'd have a chance," Zhou Yuan said, finishing the thought for her.
Su Ting said nothing.
"I'm a terrible person, aren't I?" she asked.
Zhou Yuan thought for a long time. "I don't know what's good and what's bad," he said. "I only know that five years ago you gave me to her, and five years later you wanted me back. In between, no one ever once asked me what I actually wanted."
Su Ting's face went pale. "I'm sorry."
"Don't say sorry," he said. "Neither of us is any cleaner than the other."
In April, Beijing's spring is short, but those few days were beautiful.
That evening, Su Ting met Xiaoyu at the same old bar, the same place as five years ago.
Xiaoyu looked good, her eyes bright, different from before.
"Does your mother still call?" Su Ting asked, her voice perfectly calm, her face giving nothing away.
"She calls," Xiaoyu laughed. "Last month she set me up with a doctor."
"Did you go?"
"I went. He was nice enough, but there was nothing there."
"Then forget it," Su Ting said. She drank, head down, her hand not shaking at all. Three hours earlier, she'd still been with Zhou Yuan.
"Honestly," Su Ting lit a cigarette, her voice sounding just like always, "do you regret it? The thing with Zhou Yuan."
Xiaoyu looked out the window. "I regret making him wait three years," she said. "But I don't regret leaving."
Su Ting looked at her best friend, eight years of friendship, and a sentence she couldn't make herself say out loud: actually I know exactly how he's doing, because I was with him last night.
"Was it worth it?" Su Ting heard herself ask. "For a man who ran off."
"It wasn't for him," Xiaoyu said.
"Then what was it for?"
Xiaoyu thought about it, and talked about the rain the night she turned twenty-nine, about how she was alone now but truly awake when she woke up in the mornings.
Su Ting smiled, but she knew too that the smile wasn't real.
"Here," she said, her voice not shaking at all, "to the ones who are awake."
The two glasses met.
Inside, Su Ting thought: Xiaoyu, you think you're the only person in this story who fell asleep and woke back up. You don't know there's someone else, sleeping far deeper than you ever did, who still isn't planning to wake up.
Outside, it had started raining in Beijing again.
After that night, Zhou Yuan changed.
"We can't keep doing this," he told Su Ting — and this time he meant it.
"Because of tonight?"
"Because I watched you smile and say those things to Xiaoyu," he said, "and I suddenly felt sick — not at you, at myself."
Su Ting fell silent.
"I want to tell her the truth," Zhou Yuan said.
"No." Su Ting grabbed his hand, genuinely afraid for the first time. "You can't tell her. Eight years of friendship — I can't lose her."
"Then what about us?"
"We're…" Su Ting couldn't finish the sentence.
"Is this what you want forever?" Zhou Yuan asked. "Drinking with her by day, with me by night, performing this for the rest of your life?"
"This way, I don't lose anything," Su Ting said, her voice small.
"You already have," Zhou Yuan said. "You said it yourself — that you weren't performing. But everything you're doing now is a performance."
Su Ting's tears fell. She didn't wipe them away.
"Then what do you want?" she asked.
Zhou Yuan looked at her for a long time. "I wanted someone I wouldn't have to lie to anyone for. I thought that person was you. But it turns out you're more afraid than I am of anyone seeing who you really are."
"Zhou Yuan—"
"I won't tell Xiaoyu," he said, his voice cold. "Not because I'm doing you any favors. It's because none of this concerns me anymore."
He stood up and walked out.
"You're leaving?" Su Ting asked, her voice small.
"I should have left long ago," he said. "Three years ago, five years ago — I should have left already."
A year later, Zhou Yuan moved back to a small city near his hometown, found a new job, and lived simply.
His mother was glad he was back, though she still often asked when he'd find someone.
"I'm not looking anymore," he said. "I'm fine the way I am."
He and Su Ting never spoke again.
Xiaoyu was still in Beijing. Occasionally he'd see her messages on WeChat: a new job, a trip, then another new job.
She always looked busy, always moving forward.
He never once opened Su Ting's messages. He didn't want to.
But sometimes, late at night after work, he'd think back to that year — the breakup three years ago, and the six months after that, the secret nights with Su Ting.
He wondered whether things would have been different if he hadn't gone home with Su Ting that night.
He didn't know, and didn't want to know anymore.
After that spring, Su Ting never took Xiaoyu back to that old bar in Beijing again.
She switched to a new bar, telling Xiaoyu the old place had gotten too crowded.
Only Su Ting knew the real reason: whenever she sat at that table and said "to the ones who are awake," her mind held only Zhou Yuan.
She never fell in love with anyone else either.
Sometimes Xiaoyu would ask her, "Why don't you find someone?"
"I loved someone once," Su Ting would say, smile a little, and let it drop.
Xiaoyu never pressed further. She assumed Su Ting meant some story from long ago, about some stranger.
She didn't know the man in that story was someone she'd known for years — someone she'd once nearly married.
Spring came again to Beijing.
The streets were just as crowded as ever, and no one could tell what anyone else was carrying inside.
