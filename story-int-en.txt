On the day Xiaoyu turned twenty-nine, it rained in Beijing.
She sat alone in the office. Outside the sky was black. It was already nine at night and the company was nearly empty. She still had a proposal to finish — the client would see it tomorrow morning.
Her phone rang. It was her mother.
"Xiaoyu," her mother's voice came from back home, "it's your birthday today, so I thought I'd call."
"Thanks, Mom."
"Twenty-nine," her mother said.
Xiaoyu said nothing. She knew what came next.
"You and Zhou Yuan — when exactly are you getting married?"
"Mom—"
"Listen to me. Your aunt's daughter is three years younger than you and her child is already two. Back home, when people ask me, what am I supposed to say? Where do I put my face?"
"Mom, I'm working late."
"Working late, working late — you work late every single day. Can working late produce a husband?"
Xiaoyu looked at the proposal on her screen. The words had suddenly stopped meaning anything.
"Zhou Yuan is a good man," her mother's voice softened a little. "He has a job, he's honest. What else are you looking for? You're twenty-nine. You're not young anymore."
"I know."
"Good, then," her mother said. "Next month I'll introduce you to someone. Come home and meet him."
"I'm still with Zhou Yuan."
"Then marry him!"
Xiaoyu hung up and set the phone on the desk.
The office was very quiet. Outside the window, the rain came down harder and harder.
She thought back to five years ago, when she'd just arrived in Beijing. She was twenty-four, she had nothing, and she felt she could get anything. Back then she and Su Ting shared a tiny apartment, drinking beer at night, talking about what they'd do someday.
Now she had a job, a boyfriend, an apartment that was neither big nor small.
And she sat here, hollow inside.
At eleven, Xiaoyu got home.
Zhou Yuan was watching TV in the living room. Two plates sat on the table. The food had gone cold.
"You're back," he said, without getting up.
"Sorry. Working late."
"I made you dinner."
"I ate at the office."
Zhou Yuan turned off the TV and looked at her. "Xiaoyu, can we talk properly?"
She took off her shoes and sat down beside him. "Go ahead."
"I looked at a few apartments." He took out his phone. "South side. A bit far, but cheap. I did the math — thirty-year mortgage, the two of us paying it together. We can manage it."
Xiaoyu looked at the photos on his phone. A very small apartment, its window facing someone else's wall.
"What do you think?" he asked.
"I don't know."
"What do you mean, you don't know?"
"I just…" She thought for a long time. "I just feel like maybe we're moving too fast."
Zhou Yuan laughed once — a strange laugh. "Too fast? Xiaoyu, we've been together five years."
"I know."
"Five years. You're twenty-nine this year, I'm thirty-two. What is 'too fast'?"
"That's not what I meant."
"Then what did you mean?"
Xiaoyu didn't answer.
Zhou Yuan stood and walked to the window. He kept his back to her and said nothing for a long time. Outside it was still raining.
"My mother asks me every day too," he said finally. "Asks what's going on with us. I never know what to tell her."
"So what do you want?"
"I want to marry you." He turned around. "Xiaoyu, I'm serious. I'm not clever, I don't have much money, but I'm good to you. These five years — have I been bad to you?"
"You've been very good to me."
"Then what's the problem?"
Xiaoyu looked at his face. She had looked at that face for five years; she knew every part of it. He was a good man. He really was a good man.
But a voice inside her was saying: is this your whole life, then?
She didn't say it. She said, "Let me think about it."
Zhou Yuan's face fell. "Think about it. You've been thinking about it for three years."
That night they slept back to back, a cold stretch of distance between them.
Chen Mo arrived on a Monday.
Everyone had known for a while that a new creative director was coming. But nobody knew what he looked like.
At ten in the morning, the door of the meeting room opened.
He was around thirty-five, tall, in an old black shirt, his hair a little messy. He held a cup of coffee. His face showed nothing.
"Hello, everyone. My name is Chen Mo." That was all — five words.
Then he sat down and started reading everyone's proposals.
In an hour he said fewer than twenty sentences. But every one of them landed. He saw problems no one else had seen.
Finally he picked up Xiaoyu's proposal.
"Who did this one?"
"I did," Xiaoyu said.
He lifted his head and looked at her properly for the first time.
"What's your name?"
"Lin Xiaoyu."
Chen Mo looked at her for a few seconds, then lowered his head to the proposal again.
"This proposal," he said, "is all things people want to hear. Except on page eight, where you wrote one true sentence."
The meeting room was silent.
"Which one?" Xiaoyu asked.
"'Most people buy this thing not because they need it, but because they're lonely.'" He raised his eyes. "That one's yours. Isn't it?"
Xiaoyu's face went warm. "Yes."
"Then rewrite the whole proposal as that sentence," he said. "Delete the rest."
"But the client will—"
"The client will like it." Chen Mo stood up. "Because it's true. People only respond to true things."
He walked out of the meeting room.
Su Ting came over and touched Xiaoyu's hand lightly. "That man is interesting."
Xiaoyu said nothing. She looked down and found that her hands were shaking.
That evening, Xiaoyu and Su Ting were drinking at a small bar.
Su Ting was thirty-two and had divorced last year. She lived alone now, smoked, drank, and wasn't afraid of anything.
"Something's off with you today," Su Ting said.
"I'm fine."
"Lin Xiaoyu, I've known you eight years." Su Ting lit a cigarette. "Talk."
Xiaoyu took a drink. "Zhou Yuan wants to buy an apartment."
"And?"
"And then get married."
"And you don't want to?"
Xiaoyu looked into her glass. "I don't know what I want."
Su Ting laughed. "Let me tell you something. When I got married, it was exactly like this. Everyone said: he's a good man, he's good to you, what else could you want?"
"And after?"
"After, I spent four years with a good man." Su Ting set the cigarette down. "In four years, I never once laughed as honestly as I did today."
Xiaoyu was silent for a long time.
"Do you regret it?" she asked.
"Regret getting married, or regret getting divorced?"
"Both."
Su Ting thought about it. "What I regret is that it took me four years to admit I wasn't happy. Four years, Xiaoyu. That was thirty to thirty-four. Those four years are never coming back."
Outside it started raining again.
"A new director started at the company today," Xiaoyu said suddenly.
Su Ting raised her eyes. "Oh?"
"He read my proposal. He said… he said he saw the true sentence I'd written."
"How old is he?"
"Around thirty-five."
"Married?"
Xiaoyu's hand stopped in midair. "I don't know."
Su Ting looked at her for a long, long time.
"Xiaoyu," she said finally, very quietly, "your face is red."
The proposal went through seven revisions.
On the night of the seventh, only the two of them were left in the office.
At half past eleven, Chen Mo got up from his desk and came over to her.
"Still here?"
"Almost done."
He looked at the screen, then sat down beside her. Too close. Xiaoyu could smell the faint smoke on him.
"Here," he said, pointing at the screen. "Change this word."
"To what?"
"'Ache.'"
Xiaoyu looked at the word and suddenly understood. The whole passage came alive.
"How did you think of that?" she asked.
"Because I've seen people like that," Chen Mo said.
"Like what?"
"People who have everything and ache inside."
Xiaoyu turned her head. He was right there, that close. His eyes in the light of the screen were very deep.
"Director Chen—"
"Call me Chen Mo."
"Chen Mo," she said, "why did you come to our company? Where you were before was much better than here."
He smiled slightly — the first time she had seen him smile.
"Because I'm thirty-five," he said, "and I realized I hadn't done one thing I actually loved in ten years. Meetings every day, talking every day, and not one word of it true."
"So you left."
"So I left. My wife said I'd lost my mind."
*Wife.* The word dropped between them like a stone.
Xiaoyu looked down. "You're married."
"Eight years."
"Oh."
Chen Mo was quiet for a few seconds. "Xiaoyu."
"Mm?"
"I know I shouldn't say this."
"Then don't."
"All right," he said.
But he didn't leave. He sat there with her, looking at that one word on the screen.
Outside, rain struck the window. All of Beijing was raining.
After a long while, Xiaoyu said quietly, "Me too."
"What?"
"I haven't done one thing I actually wanted to do in ten years either."
Chen Mo turned his head. This time it was him looking at her.
Neither of them said anything more.
But when his hand came to rest on the desk, one inch from hers, she didn't move hers away.
That inch took them three weeks to cross.
For three weeks, nothing happened. They ran meetings together, revised proposals together, worked late together.
And everything was different.
In the morning he'd bring her a coffee, say nothing, set it on her desk and walk away.
In afternoon meetings, he'd say one sentence and she'd already know his next.
At night she lay beside Zhou Yuan with her eyes open, thinking about someone else.
She knew exactly what this was. She also knew exactly that it was wrong.
"You've been strange lately," Zhou Yuan said.
"Work is busy."
"When you used to be busy, you'd tell me about it."
"Tell you what?"
"Tell me what you're busy with!" His voice rose. "Xiaoyu, is there something wrong with us?"
She looked at him. She wanted to say: yes, there's something wrong, and it's that I don't love you, and maybe I never really did, and I was only afraid of being alone.
She said, "No. I'm just tired."
Zhou Yuan looked at her for a long time. Then he said, "All right."
That single word. All right.
He didn't ask again that night.
But lying in the dark, Xiaoyu was truly frightened for the first time. Not of him finding out — of herself.
Because she'd discovered she didn't regret it at all.
The day the proposal was approved, the client was pleased and the company took everyone out to dinner.
Everyone drank a lot. By half past ten, people started leaving.
When Xiaoyu stepped out of the restaurant, it was pouring. She had no umbrella.
"Wait."
Chen Mo came out behind her, holding an umbrella.
"I'll walk you."
"You don't have to—"
"The rain's too heavy."
They walked into the rain together. The umbrella was small; they had to walk close. Her arm brushed his. Neither of them spoke.
At the intersection, every taxi was taken.
"My place is just ahead," Chen Mo said. "You could wait there until the rain lets up."
Xiaoyu stopped.
She knew what that sentence meant. He knew that she knew.
Rain drummed on the umbrella, loud.
"Chen Mo," she said.
"Mm."
"If I go with you now," she looked up at him, "what happens after?"
Chen Mo looked at her. There was rain on his face.
"I don't know," he said. "I could lie to you and say everything will be fine. But I don't want to lie to you."
"Then what do you want to say?"
"I want to say…" He paused. "This past year, every morning I woke up feeling like I was already dead. And the day I saw you, I found out I was still alive."
Xiaoyu's tears came suddenly. She didn't know why.
"I'm thirty-five," Chen Mo said quietly. "I'm not young, I have a wife, I can't give you anything. Walk away now and I won't say a word."
The rain kept falling.
Xiaoyu stood there and thought of her mother's phone calls, of Zhou Yuan's apartment, of the four years Su Ting talked about.
She thought of herself at twenty-four, standing on a Beijing street, certain she could have anything.
Then she reached out and took hold of his shirt.
"Stop talking," she said.
Neither of them slept that night.
Outside, the rain didn't stop until morning.
Six a.m. The sky wasn't fully light yet.
When Xiaoyu woke, Chen Mo was smoking by the window, shirtless, his back to her.
She looked at his back and suddenly felt like a stranger.
Last night this man had been the closest person in the world. Now it was light, and he'd turned back into someone else's husband.
"You're up?" He didn't turn around.
"Mm."
"I'll call you a car."
"Okay."
He pressed out the cigarette and came to sit on the edge of the bed. He started to touch her hair, then stopped halfway.
"Xiaoyu."
"Don't," she said. "Whatever you're going to say, I already know it."
"What do you know?"
"You're going to say this was a mistake. You're going to say you have a wife. You're going to say you're sorry."
Chen Mo was silent.
Then he said, "No."
Xiaoyu lifted her head.
"What I'm going to say," he looked into her eyes, "is that I don't regret it. It's a selfish thing to say. But I don't regret it."
Xiaoyu's tears came again.
"I don't regret it either," she said. "That's the frightening part."
At seven, she took a taxi home alone.
On the way she watched Beijing through the window. People heading to work, breakfast vendors, cyclists, commuters rushing for the subway. Everyone moving forward.
Her phone buzzed.
Zhou Yuan: "You didn't come home last night."
Xiaoyu looked at that line for a long time.
Then she started typing: "Let's talk."
They met at the park.
Zhou Yuan got there first. He sat on a bench holding two coffees — one of them the kind she liked.
Seeing that, something in Xiaoyu's chest twisted.
"Sit," he said.
She sat. The coffee stood between them. Neither of them picked it up.
"You first, or me?" Zhou Yuan asked.
"Why are you so calm?"
"Because I already knew." He looked at the trees ahead. "Xiaoyu, I'm not clever. But I'm not blind."
"How long have you known?"
"About a month."
"Then why didn't you ask?"
Zhou Yuan smiled a little. It was an ugly smile.
"Because I was afraid you'd tell me the truth."
The wind moved through, and the leaves sounded.
"Is it that man at your company?" he asked.
"…Yes."
"Is he married?"
"Yes."
Zhou Yuan closed his eyes for a long moment. When he opened them again, they were red.
"Xiaoyu, do you know what hurts most? It isn't you and someone else."
"Then what?"
"It's these five years." His voice shook. "For five years I thought we were fine. Every day I thought about buying an apartment, getting married, having a child someday. I thought you were thinking the same thing."
"Zhou Yuan—"
"But you weren't. You spent those five years waiting for someone to come and take you away."
Xiaoyu wanted to say it wasn't like that.
But she couldn't. Because he was right.
"I'm sorry," she said finally.
"Don't say sorry." Zhou Yuan stood. "If you were really sorry, you'd have left three years ago."
He set the coffee on the bench, turned, and walked away.
A few steps on, he stopped, without turning back.
"Xiaoyu."
"Mm."
"I hope you're really happy from now on," he said. "I'm not saying that because you left. I mean it."
Then he was gone.
Xiaoyu sat alone in the park from morning until noon. Both coffees went cold. She didn't drink a drop.
The following spring, Xiaoyu was still in Beijing.
She'd changed companies and ran a small team of her own. The salary wasn't high, but everything she made was something she wanted to make.
She and Chen Mo never ended up together.
Two months after it happened, Chen Mo's wife found out. It was ugly; everyone at the company knew. He left Beijing and went south. Before he went, he called her.
"Xiaoyu."
"Mm."
"I'm leaving."
"I know."
"Do you hate me?"
She thought for a long time. "No."
"Why not?"
"Because you never lied to me," she said. "From start to finish, you never lied to me once."
The line was quiet for a long while.
"Xiaoyu," he said at last, "you'll be all right. You're braver than I am."
After she hung up, she cried for an hour. Then she washed her face and went to work.
It was April now. Beijing's spring is short, but those few days were beautiful.
That evening she and Su Ting were drinking at the same small bar.
"Does your mother still call?" Su Ting asked.
"She calls." Xiaoyu laughed. "Last month she set me up with a doctor."
"Did you go?"
"I went. He was perfectly nice. But nothing was there."
"Then forget it."
"Mm. Forget it."
They touched glasses.
"Honestly," Su Ting lit a cigarette, "do you regret it? The Zhou Yuan thing."
Xiaoyu looked out the window. It was dark now, and the streetlights were coming on one by one.
"I regret making him wait three years," she said. "But I don't regret leaving."
"Was it worth it? For a man who ran off."
"It wasn't for him," Xiaoyu said.
"Then what was it for?"
Xiaoyu thought about it.
"The night I turned twenty-nine, I was alone in the office and it was raining outside," she said quietly. "That night I thought: this is my whole life. I'll marry a good man, buy a small apartment, pay a thirty-year mortgage, and get old."
"And now?"
"Now I'm still alone. No apartment, no boyfriend, and my mother still calls every day to scold me." She laughed. "But when I wake up in the morning, I'm actually awake."
Su Ting looked at her, and laughed too.
"Here," she said. "To the ones who are awake."
The two glasses met.
Outside, it had started raining in Beijing again.
But this time, Xiaoyu didn't feel cold at all.
